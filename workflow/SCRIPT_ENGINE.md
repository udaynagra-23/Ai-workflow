# Long-Form YouTube Script Engine

**Purpose:** Write compelling, research-grounded long-form YouTube videos that respect the viewer's time. This is the default scripting standard for the channel. Shorts are secondary adaptations.

**Operating rule:** Use a structure because it fits the story, not because a formula says every video must use it. Retention tactics must strengthen clarity, curiosity, emotion, and payoff—not manipulate or mislead.

## 1. What the research supports

The strongest practical signals from the research reviewed:
- YouTube's own guidance says the opening should match the title/thumbnail promise. Its retention report identifies intro performance at 30 seconds, top moments, spikes, and dips; use those observations to improve future videos.
- YouTube recommends testing different opening approaches and comparing retention with the creator's own videos of similar length.
- Creator guidance supports opening with a question, an immediate demonstration, or a cold open rather than delaying the subject with a generic intro.
- Common narrative frameworks—central mystery, chronological story, rise/fall, parallel threads, causal "but/therefore" progression, and nested questions—are useful planning tools. They are not guaranteed performance hacks.
- Fixed rules such as "interrupt every 20 seconds" or "always re-hook at exactly 50%" are not reliable universal laws. Use natural story turns and actual audience data instead.

## 2. Required intake and packaging promise

Before outlining:
1. Define the target viewer in one sentence: what they know, want, wonder about, or misunderstand.
2. Define the video's central promise: what will the viewer understand or discover by the end?
3. Draft a provisional title/thumbnail concept. It is a promise test, not a final lock.
4. Make sure the first 30 seconds visibly and verbally deliver on that promise.
5. Capture the user's own ideas, examples, boundaries, and opinions. Never replace them with invented preferences.
6. Set a realistic target runtime and narration style.

If a critical choice is missing, make a clearly labelled safe assumption where possible. Ask the user only when the missing decision materially changes the story.

## 3. Select the story structure

Choose one primary structure. A hybrid is allowed when it improves clarity.

| Structure | Use when | Core movement |
|---|---|---|
| Central question / mystery | A question, puzzle, surprising result, or disputed explanation drives the topic | Question → clues/evidence → complications → strongest explanation → answer |
| Chronological story with cold open | Events unfold in meaningful time order | Start at a consequential moment → rewind for context → follow causes and consequences → return to opening moment |
| Rise and fall | A person, company, technology, movement, or system changes dramatically | Conditions for rise → growth → hidden weaknesses → turning point → decline/legacy |
| Parallel threads that converge | Two or more people, inventions, events, or forces influence each other | Introduce threads → develop separately → reveal connection → convergence and consequence |
| Problem / mechanism / consequence | An explanatory or educational topic | Everyday question/problem → how the system works → evidence/examples → consequences/trade-offs → clear answer |
| Transformation / journey | A real person or project changes through decisions and obstacles | Starting state → goal → obstacles → decisions and setbacks → changed state and lesson |

### Causal scene connection: But / Therefore
Use this at the paragraph, beat, or chapter level:
- "But" introduces a complication, contradiction, obstacle, or new evidence.
- "Therefore" shows a consequence, decision, or logical result.
- Avoid a chain of unrelated "and then" facts.
- Do not force conflict where the real subject is calm or descriptive; causality and discovery are enough.

## 4. Build the opening

The opening is a compact contract with the viewer:
1. **Immediate subject:** show the exact person, object, event, question, or result promised by the packaging.
2. **Tension or curiosity:** present a contradiction, unresolved question, consequence, or surprising result.
3. **Stakes:** explain why the question matters.
4. **Promise:** make clear what the video will answer or reveal.
5. **Forward pull:** establish one meaningful question that the story will resolve.

Opening options:
- Cold open: begin inside the most consequential moment.
- Question: pose a specific question the video can genuinely answer.
- Result first: show the outcome, then investigate how it happened.
- Visual demonstration: show the phenomenon before explaining it.
- Contradiction: place two apparently incompatible facts side by side.

Avoid:
- Generic "Hey guys, welcome back" introductions before the subject.
- Long logo sequences or channel housekeeping.
- Empty superlatives, fake urgency, fabricated stakes, or a hook the video cannot fulfil.
- Teasing a reveal that is not actually delivered.

## 5. Design chapters as mini-stories

Use the number of chapters the subject needs; 3–6 is a useful starting range, not a hard rule.

Each chapter should contain:
1. A specific question, claim, or objective.
2. Concrete beats: an event, example, source, demonstration, or piece of evidence.
3. Explanation: why the beat matters.
4. A turn, discovery, consequence, or partial answer.
5. A bridge that makes the next chapter feel necessary.

Chapter titles should communicate the story's idea or question, not generic labels such as "Background" or "Part 2".

Vary the rhythm naturally:
- Alternate explanation with concrete scenes, examples, demonstrations, and visual breathing room.
- Follow dense information with a clear example or short recap.
- Use a visual change when the information, place, point of view, or emotional beat changes.
- Do not add random effects or cutaways merely to hit a timer.

## 6. Use open loops responsibly

Maintain a small written loop tracker so promises are not forgotten.

| Loop ID | Question opened | Opened in | Planned answer/payoff | Closed in | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Rules:
- Keep only a few meaningful questions active at once.
- Close questions clearly; do not leave the central promise unresolved.
- A chapter may answer one question while naturally raising the next.
- Revisit an earlier clue or image when it creates a satisfying payoff.
- Never manufacture a mystery, withhold a simple answer artificially, or use repeated fake cliffhangers.
- A loop is optional. A clear explanation can be compelling without suspense.

## 7. Plan the middle and re-hooks

The middle should deepen the story, not repeat the introduction.

At major natural turning points, consider:
- A surprising piece of evidence.
- A contradiction that changes the viewer's understanding.
- A consequence that raises the stakes.
- A shift in scale, location, time, or point of view.
- A concise recap followed by the next unresolved question.

A midpoint re-hook can help if the story has a genuine second act or turning point. Do not force a twist or place one at a fixed percentage in every video. Re-hooks should be earned by the material.

## 8. Research and evidence before persuasive narration

Before drafting factual claims:
- Build a research brief with source links and exact claim notes.
- Prefer primary sources: original documents, official datasets, direct statements, scientific papers, technical documentation, and first-hand records.
- Use reputable independent reporting and expert sources to add context and challenge assumptions.
- Cross-check consequential claims; distinguish established facts, estimates, disputed claims, and interpretation.
- Check dates, names, units, quotations, and visual context.
- Record uncertainty and relevant counter-evidence.
- Never invent dialogue, scenes, statistics, citations, or access to events.
- Keep a source note beside the script section it supports.

## 9. Write for the ear

The script is meant to be heard, not silently admired:
- Use natural spoken language and concrete verbs.
- Keep most sentences easy to follow on first hearing.
- Give each paragraph one main idea.
- Explain specialist terms before relying on them.
- Avoid repeated conclusions, filler phrases, and inflated adjectives.
- Read difficult passages aloud or use a narration test when available.
- Add pronunciation notes for names and unfamiliar terms.
- Vary sentence length for emphasis, but do not make every line artificially dramatic.

Estimate runtime from the actual narrator's measured speaking pace when possible. Record a short sample, calculate that narrator's words per minute, and use it for the first timing estimate. Recalculate after pauses, visual holds, demonstrations, and dialogue are included. Do not treat a generic words-per-minute figure as a universal rule.

## 10. Write visuals alongside narration

Every script section must include a visual intention:
- What should the viewer see at this exact moment?
- Does the visual prove, explain, locate, compare, humanize, or emotionally reinforce the narration?
- Is the best visual real footage, an archival image, a diagram, a timeline, a map, a chart, a screen recording, or a deliberate pause?
- What source, rights, attribution, or accuracy checks are required?
- Which approved scene and animation template IDs can be reused?

Do not use random stock footage as a substitute for explaining the subject. Do not make a visual imply something the evidence does not establish. Keep important labels readable and on screen long enough to understand.

## 11. Ending and CTA

- Resolve the opening promise explicitly.
- Explain what the evidence supports and what remains uncertain.
- Give the viewer a meaningful final takeaway, consequence, or perspective.
- Echo an image, phrase, or question from the opening only if it feels natural.
- Use at most one relevant CTA. It is optional; the ending should not be weakened by a generic subscription request.
- If teasing another video, make it a genuine next question rather than an unrelated sales pitch.

## 12. Required script deliverables

Every long-form script package must include:
1. Target viewer and viewer promise.
2. Provisional title/thumbnail promise.
3. Chosen story structure and why it fits.
4. Research brief and source list.
5. Chapter outline with each chapter's purpose.
6. Full narration script.
7. Scene-by-scene visual directions, on-screen text, and audio notes.
8. Open-loop tracker (or explicitly state that the story does not need open loops).
9. Runtime estimate based on narrator pace and visual holds.
10. Fact-check and narration-readability checklist.
11. A short note listing assumptions and unresolved uncertainties.

## 13. Independent script QA

Before storyboard approval, review the script as a critical editor:
- Does the title/thumbnail promise match the actual content?
- Does the first half-minute make the subject and promise clear?
- Is there one central question or clear learning objective?
- Does the selected structure fit the material?
- Does each chapter advance the central question?
- Are causal connections clear rather than a list of facts?
- Are claims sourced and uncertainty represented?
- Are any loops forgotten, misleading, or artificially prolonged?
- Does the middle introduce meaningful development?
- Does the ending answer the opening promise?
- Does the narration sound natural aloud?
- Does every visual have a job?
- Can any section be shortened without losing meaning?
- Are there repeated explanations, unsupported claims, filler, or generic AI-sounding phrases?

Revise, then recheck the revised script. Report remaining concerns honestly.

## 14. Post-publish learning loop

When analytics become available, review each video after YouTube has processed its retention report:
- Intro: inspect the first 30 seconds and whether it matched the title/thumbnail promise.
- Flat sections/top moments: identify what viewers stayed for; consider whether that kind of content belongs earlier or deserves a follow-up.
- Dips: inspect what happened at that moment—slow setup, unclear explanation, repetition, abrupt transition, or mismatch.
- Spikes: determine whether viewers rewatched because the moment was compelling or because it was confusing.
- Compare with the channel's own videos of similar length and its typical retention; avoid treating a single video or universal benchmark as a verdict.
- Convert repeated, supported findings into script or editing improvements. Keep one-off anomalies as observations, not permanent rules.

YouTube notes that retention processing can take around 1–2 days and that key-moment highlights may require at least 100 views. If data is unavailable or too limited, say so and use qualitative review instead.
